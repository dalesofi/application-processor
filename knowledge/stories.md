## IRONHACK_B2B_ENGINE

### Short description
Built a more repeatable B2B product engine that allowed a small team to turn different customer requirements into scalable, reusable product solutions rather than building bespoke products from scratch.

### Context
At Ironhack, the B2B business was growing and customers often had different requirements. The challenge was to move from a model where individual customer needs could easily become bespoke projects toward a more structured and repeatable product approach.

The broader company operated across multiple international markets, growing from roughly 3 campuses when I joined to 11+ locations.

### Problem
B2B customers had different needs and requirements. Without enough structure, this could lead to:
- bespoke solutions
- repeated work
- slow delivery
- inconsistent experiences
- difficulty scaling the product operation

The challenge was to create enough standardisation to move quickly while still allowing meaningful customer-specific variation.

### What I did
- Worked closely with customers and internal stakeholders to understand users, problems and requirements.
- Established clearer workflows and communication cadences between the product team and customers.
- Created a more reusable approach to product development using a product library / reusable product components.
- Used learning and patterns from B2C products to inform B2B solutions where appropriate.
- Worked across product, design, engineering, operations and commercial teams.
- Helped introduce more structure, predictability and efficiency into the product process.
- Continued to own product strategy, discovery, prioritisation and delivery across international markets.

### Outcome
- Enabled a small team to handle different customer requirements in a more repeatable way.
- Increased standardisation and predictability while still supporting customer needs.
- Contributed to operational improvements that reduced operational costs by approximately 30%.
- Supported the company's ability to operate productively across 11+ international markets.

### What this story proves
- B2B product experience
- Product systems thinking
- Standardisation vs customisation
- Scaling product operations
- Customer discovery
- Cross-functional leadership
- Working with ambiguity
- Operational efficiency
- Reusable product thinking
- International product environments

### Best used for
- B2B SaaS
- Platform products
- Enterprise products
- Product Operations
- Technical/product systems roles
- Scaling organisations
- Operational efficiency
- Customer-specific product environments
- Roles requiring stakeholder management

### Avoid / limitations

Do not claim direct ownership of revenue unless supported by the specific application context.

## FIGMA_SIMPLIFICATION

### Short description
Simplified Ironhack's learning workflow by moving teams toward Figma as the central design tool instead of maintaining a more fragmented multi-tool workflow.

### Context
During COVID, Ironhack had to move the learning experience online very quickly. The existing experience relied on multiple tools and increasingly complex documentation and workflows.

This created unnecessary complexity for students and teachers.

### Problem
The product team could have responded by adding more functionality and documentation.

Instead, the question became:

> Could we simplify the experience by reducing the number of tools?

### Discovery
I:
- Talked to students and teachers.
- Reviewed class recordings.
- Looked at usage behaviour and feedback.
- Looked at the broader market/tooling context.
- Noticed that people were already naturally sharing Figma links and using Figma in the workflow.

This suggested that Figma could become the centre of the design experience rather than one tool among many.

### What I did
- Built the product case for standardising the design workflow around Figma.
- Got leadership buy-in.
- Designed and ran pilots.
- Organised review rounds and gathered feedback.
- Trained teams.
- Managed rollout across locations.
- Monitored adoption and reported progress to leadership.
- Iterated based on feedback and usage.

### Outcome
- Went from initial idea to testing with the first cohorts in approximately one month.
- Reached full adoption in the first quarter.
- Maintained approximately 4.5 satisfaction.
- Reduced complexity for students and teachers.
- Reduced tooling/maintenance complexity and associated costs.

### What this story proves
- UX-to-product thinking
- User research
- Behavioural evidence
- Simplification
- Product judgment
- Change management
- Experimentation/piloting
- Cross-functional leadership
- Adoption
- Operational thinking
- Knowing when NOT to add functionality

### Best used for
- UX/Product roles
- Core UX
- Consumer products
- Product simplification
- Growth through better experience
- Platform/tool consolidation
- Change management
- Product judgment interviews
- “Tell me about a product you improved”
- “Tell me about a difficult product decision”

### Core lesson
Improving a core experience does not always mean adding capability. Sometimes the highest-impact product decision is removing complexity and giving users one clear path.

## ADVANCED_UI_FAILURE

### Short description
A product launch where I correctly identified and validated the problem but over-invested in a solution before sufficiently validating the solution and willingness to pay.

### Context
At Button School, we developed an Advanced UI product based on a problem we believed was real and validated.

The mistake was moving too quickly from validated problem to a relatively sophisticated solution.

### Problem
We spent approximately four months building the product, including significant investment in a sophisticated LMS.

The problem was not necessarily the underlying customer need. The problem was that we had not validated:
1. whether the proposed solution was the right one
2. whether customers would pay for it at the expected level

### What I did
After the initial launch showed weak sales:
- Analysed what was happening.
- Recognised that we had overbuilt before testing the solution properly.
- Ran a more constrained pilot.
- Used discounts to reduce the barrier to trial.
- Worked closely with customers during delivery.
- Gathered direct feedback.
- Used the pilot to understand where the product actually created value.

### Outcome
The product continues to sell, but it performed below the expectations created by the initial investment.

The most important outcome was the product lesson rather than a commercial success.

### What I learned
Problem validation, solution validation and willingness-to-pay validation are three different things.

A validated problem does not automatically validate the solution.

For future products I would:
- prototype earlier
- test the smallest viable solution
- validate willingness to pay sooner
- avoid building sophisticated infrastructure before enough evidence exists

### What this story proves
- Product judgment
- Intellectual honesty
- Learning from failure
- Experimentation
- Commercial thinking
- MVP/prototyping mindset
- Willingness to challenge your own assumptions
- Understanding of product risk

### Best used for
- “Tell me about a failure”
- “Tell me about a product you would do differently”
- Product judgment interviews
- Experimentation
- 0→1
- Startup environments
- AI/product roles
- Questions about mistakes or lessons learned

### Important framing
Do not try to turn this into a success story.

The value of the story is precisely that the product underperformed and the lesson changed how I approach validation.

Built a small independent product using AI-assisted development to explore how a calendar could become a tool for making better decisions about how to spend time.

### Context
I wanted to explore a personal product idea around time management and the gap between having a calendar and actually making intentional decisions about how time is spent.

### Problem
Calendars mostly show what is scheduled.

The experiment was to explore whether a product could help interpret time through personal priorities/principles rather than simply displaying appointments.

### What I did
- Defined the initial product problem myself.
- Used AI-assisted coding tools, primarily Cursor and LLMs/ChatGPT.
- Used AI throughout the process to explore the problem, structure the solution, generate and debug code, prototype the experience and iterate.
- Made product and UX decisions myself rather than simply prompting AI to build something.
- Used the low cost of AI-assisted development to move from idea to functional prototype quickly.
- Iterated on the product through building and testing.

### Outcome
Built a functional independent product/prototype called `im-time`.

### What this story proves

- Product thinking

# EVIDENCE RULES

When generating applications, interviews or CV content from these stories:

1. Never invent a metric.
2. Never turn approximate numbers into exact numbers.
3. Never claim ownership where the story only establishes contribution.
4. Distinguish "I led" from "I contributed to."
5. Do not convert a pilot into a full-scale launch.
6. Do not convert a prototype into a production system.
7. Do not claim domain expertise from adjacent experience.
8. Prefer a specific real story over a generic competency statement.
9. A failure can be used when the question asks about learning, judgment or mistakes.
10. If a stronger story exists, do not force a weaker one simply because it contains a metric.
